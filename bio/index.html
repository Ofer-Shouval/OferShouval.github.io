A Brooklyn-based musician, visual artist, and educator whose albums, live shows and interactive installations explore the interplay between people and the machines we live with.

< music >

Under the name O. Wake, Shouval writes, arranges, performs, and produces conceptual albums that are cinematic in scope, sweeping listeners into vivid stories that comment on the state of the world.

His debut album, Unfamiliar State (2024), is a descent into algorithmic hell that ends in a meditation on acceptance, “Just Keep On Living in the Moment.” It draws on psychedelia, post-punk and dance music: riff-driven guitars ride over grooves built for dancing, woven through with computer-programmed elements, with the lyrics at the center.

One of the best kept secrets of the Brooklyn music scene, the album is proof, in BandsDoBK’s words, that “music that dares to go deep can also make you dance.” Nonewwavenofun called it “unashamedly conceptual in its appeal” and “one of the most fascinating records I’ve heard in years.”

The next record, due in early 2028, is darker and more personal: an album about what it means to belong, and what happens to a person when that sense of belonging disintegrates.

< visual art >

Shouval’s interest in creative technology and interactive art started in service of his music. Recognizing the power of immersive experiences to draw an audience into a shared moment, he developed a creative practice of building the tools and media that carry his songs beyond the sound.

The most ambitious of those tools is the Luminin, the visual instrument he built for his MFA thesis in Interactive Art at Pratt Institute (2021). Most light shows are programmed in advance or controlled by someone offstage, leaving the musician with no hand in what the audience sees. He wanted one he could play live, with all the risk and life that live playing brings. A motion sensor on his guitar’s headstock reads every tilt, swing and jolt, and turns them into light as he plays. The light is meant to work the way a film score does: it serves the song rather than competing with it.

O. Wake’s shows became its testing ground, from the band’s own hand-built LED screens to the house lights of Baby’s All Right at the Unfamiliar State release show. But the Luminin was never meant for one band alone. Built from cheap, easy-to-find parts, it’s a step toward a tool that gives working musicians a dynamic, affordable light show of their own. That goal still drives his work: instruments and experiences that turn people’s movement into something they can see and feel, and pull them out of their screens.

< teaching >

That same impulse, to put tools in other people’s hands, carries into his teaching. He teaches creative coding and interaction design at NYU, and interactive media and audio for digital media at Pratt, where he also looks after the modular synth lab. For his Pratt animation students, he is building a curriculum that demystifies sound design and teaches them to listen critically to the world around them. He has also taught physical computing in the graduate Design & Technology program at Parsons School of Design.

His classes draw on a knowledge base as broad as his practice: code, electronics, sound, video and fabrication. Each one starts with a crash course in the tools, then turns to the question that matters most: what do you want to make? From there, his job is to help students bring their own ideas to life, the part of teaching he loves most.
